# Chapter 2: Space Weather and the 14 MHz Silence

Inside the radio shack, temperature is maintained at an exact equilibrium: warm
enough that your fingers can operate a telegraph key without stiffening, but
cool enough that the thermionic glow of the equipment feels earned.

On my screen, a clean text terminal displayed the custom *Rymdväder* space
weather dashboard. While the rest of humanity checks weather forecasts to decide
whether to carry an umbrella, radio amateurs check the weather 150 million
kilometers away to decide whether electromagnetic waves can bounce off the
F2-layer of the ionosphere or get incinerated in the D-layer.

For three days, the solar disk had been relatively well-behaved. The sunspot
number was 142, the 10.7 cm Solar Flux Index was purring at a comfortable 160,
and the geomagnetic K-index sat at a tranquil 1. 

On 14.025 MHz—the sweet spot of the twenty-meter amateur band—the world was
ordinarily alive with crisp, melodic Morse signals: coastal beacons in Norway,
maritime mobile stations somewhere west of Gibraltar, and polite operators in
Japan signing off with *TU 73 ES ARIGATO*.

I adjusted my headphones, leaned forward, and clicked on the terminator tool
(*Termin*) to check the twilight grey-line. The dark edge of night was just
sweeping across the North Atlantic—the golden hour for long-distance low-power
contacts.

Then, without warning, the background hiss in the headphones died.

It did not fade out gradually like a train receding into the spruce forests. It
vanished completely, as if someone had snipped the antenna wire with silver
scissors. The receiver was stone silent. S-meter: flat zero.

I checked the dashboard. A coronal mass ejection (CME) had just collided with
Earth's magnetosphere. The K-index, previously sleeping peacefully at 1, had
spiked straight to 7. 

"Ah," I said to the empty room. "Space has arrived."

Outside the window, above the tops of the frosted pine trees, the sky was
beginning to shimmer with pale emerald and violet curtains of Aurora Borealis.
Tourists pay thousands of kronor to fly into the Arctic circle and photograph the
Northern Lights while shivering inside rented goose-down parkas.

Radio amateurs, on the other hand, look at the Northern Lights and see a giant
celestial wall of charged plasma laughing at their transmissions.

When the ionosphere closes for business, there is only one civilized response:
step back from the radio, put the kettle on, slice a piece of kardemummabulle,
and wait for the Sun to regain its composure. Solar physics cannot be hurried,
especially on a Saturday evening.
