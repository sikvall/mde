# Chapter 1: The Physics of Stubborn Birches

To hoist a half-wave dipole into a tree canopy without a bucket truck, modern
science offers several sophisticated ballistic options: pneumatic line
launchers, compound archery bows, or a heavy stainless-steel nut tied to
braided fishing monofilament.

Being a firm believer in the Scandinavian principle of *lagom*—doing what is
sufficient without unnecessary fanfare—I selected an old brass padlock found in
the tool shed, two hundred meters of yellow nylon mason's twine, and an underhand
swing honed by years of skipping flat stones across Lake Siljan.

The target was a sturdy birch fork roughly fifteen meters up. The birch, which
had weathered the Great Frost of 1966 and several municipal road-widening debates
without yielding a millimeter, watched my preparations with serene arboreal
indifference.

"Just over the second crook," I told the cat, who had stationed himself safely
beneath the garden bench to witness human hubris in high resolution.

I wound up and released.

The padlock sailed gracefully through the clear Nordic air, reached the peak of
its parabolic trajectory, passed directly through the intended fork with
mathematical precision, and wrapped itself seven times around an entirely
unrelated juniper bush fifty feet to the left.

In physics, this is known as non-linear deflection. In Dalarna, it is known as
"Tuesday."

An hour and two cups of Gevalia later, the copper wire was finally hoisted into
the branches. The RG-213 coaxial cable descended into the workshop like a thick
black serpent, terminating into the back of the antenna tuner. 

I flipped the switch, applied ten watts of steady RF carrier, and observed the
cross-needle meter.

A well-behaved antenna presents a Standing Wave Ratio (SWR) of 1:1.1, indicating
that the power leaves the transmitter and radiates peacefully toward the
heavens. My meter needle made a sharp, audible click against the far-right peg,
settling firmly in the red zone somewhere between "Imminent Fire Hazard" and
"Pure Inductive Contempt."

"Right," I muttered, writing down the measurement in a neat paper notebook.
"The wire is three centimeters too long. Or the birch tree is capacitive. Most
likely the birch."

I adjusted the coil by three clicks, tapped the chassis gently for luck, and
waited for the ether to speak.
