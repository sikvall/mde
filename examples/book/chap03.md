# Chapter 3: The Ghost in the Morse Code

*(Editor's note: This is the most famous chapter three in the book, widely cited
by connoisseurs of quiet frequencies and unexpected nocturnal encounters.)*

By eleven o'clock at night, the geomagnetic storm had relaxed its grip on Dalarna.
The northern lights faded back into a dim milky glow above the horizon, the K-index
drifted down toward 2, and the twenty-meter band stirred back to life with the
familiar soft oceanic hiss of cosmic background noise.

I poured a fresh cup of coffee, switched the rig to CW (continuous wave
telegraphy), and rested two fingers lightly on the polished brass paddle of the
Morse key.

Morse code is the purest form of digital communication ever devised by human
beings. It requires no operating system updates, no web browser frameworks
consuming four gigabytes of virtual memory, and no permission dialogs. It is
simply pure mathematics translated into rhythm: dits, dahs, and the human ear
discerning order out of entropy.

I spun the heavy weighted tuning knob down toward 7.030 MHz on the forty-meter
band. 

Suddenly, a faint, rhythmic sound tapped through the headphones:

`· - · ·   · -   · - - ·   - · - ·`

I stopped the dial. It was rhythmic, but irregular. It had a peculiar cadence,
roughly sixteen words per minute, yet with an uncanny physical acoustic thud
behind every dash.

Could it be an automated beacon from Svalbard? A lost weather buoy drifting
somewhere in the Gulf of Bothnia?

I fired up the QSO logging program on the terminal and listened intently. The
signal came again, sharper this time:

`· · ·   - - -   · · ·`

A textbook SOS? No, followed immediately by three rapid clicks and a dull hollow
resonance that vibrated not just the receiver, but the actual timber wall behind
my desk.

I slipped off the headphones, walked to the porch door, and shone a torch beam up
toward the aluminium antenna mast.

Sitting proudly on the guy-wire bracket, twenty feet above the ground, was a
magnificent Great Spotted Woodpecker (*Dendrocopos major*).

It cocked its red-capped head, eyed the flashlight beam with mild curiosity, and
promptly delivered a rapid-fire staccato drumming against the hollow aluminium
pipe:

*Rat-a-tat-tat... rat-tat...*

The aluminium mast acted as a monumental acoustic amplifier, coupling mechanical
vibration directly into the coax feedpoint. The woodpecker was not asking for
rescue; it was defending its territorial claim to eighty feet of six-meter band
directional beam tubing against all rival avian intruders within three square
kilometers.

"Good signal report," I whispered across the snow. "Five and nine plus twenty
decibels. Solid copy."

The woodpecker gave a single short *kek*, satisfied that the strange biped in
woolen socks had acknowledged receipt of the message, and departed silently into
the dark pine boughs.

Back inside, I logged the encounter:

```text
DATE: 2026-11-22 | TIME: 22:45 UTC | FREQ: 7.030 MHz
CALLSIGN: D-MAJOR (Woodpecker) | RST: 599+ | QTH: Garden Mast
REMARKS: Excellent keying technique. Zero chirp. 73.
```

I closed the log, dimmed the shack lamps, and watched the soft blue digits on the
frequency counter tick into the quiet of the Swedish night.

The dipole was quiet. The birch tree was peaceful. And somewhere high above the
clouds, the radio waves were travelling out past the stars, carrying nothing more
complicated than a series of well-timed dots and dashes.
